# The tracks

*The four appointment families in Arts & Sciences, how their ranks correspond, and what moving between them takes. Source: Appendix II, Contract-Based Faculty Guidelines. Content last checked 21 September 2026.*

Four kinds of faculty appointment run in parallel here. They are not four rungs of one ladder, which is the single most common misreading, and it matters because two of them have ranks you can be promoted through and two of them do not.

## Instructional track

The instructional track is the University's ranked, contract-based, non-tenure-eligible faculty line. Appendix II also calls it IT, and the titles carry the words *of instruction*: Assistant Professor of Instruction, Associate Professor of Instruction, Professor of Instruction. These are experts in delivering strong educational experiences, and the guidelines describe their effort as dedicated to instructional activities.

What the track is evaluated on is the part worth memorizing, because it is where the surprise lives. There are three pillars, and instructional work is only the first of them.

Instructional work is the obvious one: teaching, tutoring, mentoring students, developing and redesigning courses and curricula and educational materials, grounding the work in current pedagogical practice, creating an environment where students grow, and reflecting on your own practice.

Service is the second, and it is broad enough to be worth reading slowly. Committee work at department, school, or University level. Participating in governance. Student academic and career advising. Advising a student group, team, or internship program. Faculty mentoring. Linking the professional skills of faculty and students to the world beyond campus. Representing the University in public forums or with the media. Service to your own professional societies, including organizing conferences or holding a leadership role in a professional organization. And one clause that reads like it was written for an operational role: improving program quality, reputation, and operational efficiency. Service expectations vary by department and get established in the contract and in conversation with the chair or director, who is also charged with making sure service expectations and opportunities are distributed equitably across a unit.

Other engagement is the third, defined as anything that directly or indirectly enriches the pedagogical work. Scholarship and creative work sit here, along with advising, administrative work, program assessment, professional development such as Teaching Center workshops, conference attendance, community-engaged projects, and investigating specialized knowledge as it bears on your teaching.

All three pillars are supposed to be documented as you go, in annual activity reports and chair reports, and then assembled into a portfolio when a promotion case is built. The annual report is not a formality; it is the evidentiary base that a later review reads.

The ranks and their contracts run like this. An assistant professor of instruction starts on a three-year contract by default, and the first renewal requires a full reappointment review rather than the streamlined one. After that first review, subsequent assistant-level contracts default to four years and the streamlined process becomes available. Promotion eligibility arrives after six years at rank, or equivalent experience. An associate professor of instruction holds a five-year default contract, reappoints through the streamlined process as long as the annual activity reports have been reviewed satisfactorily by the chair or director, and becomes promotion-eligible after another six years at rank or equivalent. A full professor of instruction is also on five years with streamlined reappointment. A shorter contract than the default is permitted only in limited circumstances, which the guidelines name specifically: either the position was intentionally built as a shorter-term job, or there are documented performance concerns.

The track also carries governance rights, and they are more extensive than people assume. Instructional-track faculty are full participants in the undergraduate affairs of their departments. They are eligible for departmental faculty meetings, academic program committees, student recruitment, hiring committees where the hire is instructional-track, and promotion committees where the person under review is instructional-track. Faculty Council opens up after three years of service, the same threshold as tenure-track. The College Administrative Committee, the College Curriculum Committee, and the College Committee on Interdisciplinary Programs all open after the initial three-year appointment, and the College Board on academic honesty is open immediately. Graduate work is possible with a lean toward master's-level training rather than doctoral mentorship, and sitting on a PhD qualifying exam or thesis defense requires petitioning the University Office of Graduate Education and Postdoctoral Affairs first. Doctoral committees are otherwise closed absent an exception from the University Dean of Graduate Studies.

One thing the guidelines say plainly and it is easy to miss: staying at rank is allowed. An instructional-track faculty member may decline to pursue promotion without negative consequence, and may go up, not get it, continue at rank under the normal reappointment rules, and try again later.

## Teaching track

Lecturer, senior lecturer, and full-time instructor are the teaching-track titles. Appendix II groups them as full-time faculty with primary teaching responsibilities and describes them as holding *functional* titles, which is the tell: they are job descriptions rather than ranks. There is no promotion ladder inside the teaching track. You hold the title until something moves you off it.

The defining sentence, and the one with real consequences, is that these individuals are hired to teach for a limited term without expectation of other service to the department or University. Read against the instructional track, where service is one of three evaluation pillars, that is a substantial difference in what the appointment is understood to be. Work that would be evidence on one track is outside the stated scope of the other.

Faculty holding research appointments, such as scientist or engineer, may teach and supervise undergraduate research in their areas, and those contributions get recognized by adding the title of lecturer. So the word lecturer also appears as a secondary title on an appointment whose primary line is research.

## Adjunct

Adjunct appointments are for teaching contracted on a per-course basis. The guidelines also point at a second, less obvious use: an adjunct appointment suits someone with an independent professional career where a formal affiliation benefits both the department and the individual. That is the mechanism for keeping a working professional attached to a program without pretending the arrangement is a faculty line.

## Tenure track

The tenure track is a separate family and Appendix II does not govern it. Its reappointment, tenure, and promotion rules live in Appendix III. Worth knowing the boundary exists, because Appendix II repeatedly contrasts itself against the tenure track and it is easy to read one document's rules as the University's only rules.

## Conversion between tracks

Moving from the teaching track to the instructional track is called conversion, not promotion, and the mapping is more generous than the titles suggest.

A senior lecturer can be considered for conversion directly to associate professor of instruction, skipping the assistant rank entirely, and can subsequently be considered for promotion to professor of instruction. A lecturer or full-time instructor can be considered for conversion to assistant professor of instruction, and subsequently for promotion to associate. So the University already treats senior lecturer as roughly mid-ladder rather than entry-level.

Three things gate a conversion: appropriate length of service, a positive review, and a recommendation from the department chair. The chair's recommendation is the part that cannot be routed around.

Two further details decide how a conversion actually plays out. The first is that conversion is described as an administrative process, which is to say it is not a competitive case built against external letters and a national field — it is a reclassification of an existing appointment. The second is the sentence that follows it: conversion does not automatically carry a salary increase, and an increase would need to be justified separately. Those two facts pull in opposite directions and both are true. The title change is comparatively cheap to execute. The money is a separate argument, with a separate evidentiary burden, and nothing about the conversion makes it for you.
